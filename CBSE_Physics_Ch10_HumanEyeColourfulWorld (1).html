01 · THE HUMAN EYE

The Human Eye — Structure & Function

👁️

The Eye — A Camera

The human eye is like a camera. Its lens system forms an image on a light-sensitive screen called the retina. The eyeball is approximately spherical with a diameter of about 2.3 cm.

🔬

Cornea

Light enters through a thin membrane called the cornea — the transparent bulge on the front surface. Most refraction for entering light rays occurs at the outer surface of the cornea.

⚙️

Crystalline Lens

The crystalline lens provides finer adjustment of focal length to focus objects at different distances on the retina. It is composed of a fibrous, jelly-like material.

💡

Retina & Optic Nerve

The retina is a delicate membrane with enormous light-sensitive cells. These generate electrical signals sent to the brain via the optic nerve. The brain interprets these signals.

01.1 · POWER OF ACCOMMODATION

Power of Accommodation

1
Distant Objects — Ciliary Muscles Relax

When muscles are relaxed, the lens becomes thin. Focal length increases. This enables clear vision of distant objects.

2
Near Objects — Ciliary Muscles Contract

Looking at objects closer to the eye, ciliary muscles contract. Curvature of eye lens increases — lens becomes thicker. Focal length decreases. Enables clear near vision.

3
Near Point — Least Distance of Distinct Vision

The minimum distance at which objects can be seen most distinctly without strain. For a young adult with normal vision: 25 cm. Also called the near point of the eye.

4
Far Point of the Eye

The farthest point up to which the eye can see objects clearly. It is infinity for a normal eye. A normal eye sees objects clearly between 25 cm and infinity.

⚠️ Cataract: Sometimes the crystalline lens of people at old age becomes milky and cloudy. This condition is called cataract. It causes partial or complete loss of vision. Vision can be restored through cataract surgery.

02 · DEFECTS OF VISION

Defects of Vision & Their Correction

🔴
Myopia (Near-sightedness)

What: Can see nearby objects clearly but cannot see distant objects distinctly.

Cause 1: Excessive curvature of the eye lens.

Cause 2: Elongation of the eyeball.

Effect: Image of distant object formed in front of the retina.

Far point: Nearer than infinity.

✅ Correction: Concave lens (diverging) of suitable power
🟡
Hypermetropia (Far-sightedness)

What: Can see distant objects clearly but cannot see nearby objects distinctly.

Cause 1: Focal length of eye lens too long.

Cause 2: Eyeball has become too small.

Effect: Image of near object formed behind the retina.

Near point: Farther than normal 25 cm.

✅ Correction: Convex lens (converging) of suitable power
🟣
Presbyopia

What: Power of accommodation decreases with ageing. Near point gradually recedes.

Cause: Gradual weakening of ciliary muscles and diminishing flexibility of the eye lens.

Effect: Difficulty seeing nearby objects without corrective eye-glasses.

Note: A person may suffer from both myopia and hypermetropia → requires bi-focal lenses.

✅ Correction: Bi-focal lenses (concave upper + convex lower)

03 · REFRACTION THROUGH A PRISM

Refraction of Light Through a Prism

🔷 Structure of a Prism

A triangular glass prism has two triangular bases and three rectangular lateral surfaces. These surfaces are inclined to each other.

📐 Angle of the Prism (∠A): The angle between its two lateral refracting faces.

📌 Key difference from glass slab: In a glass slab, the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray (parallel surfaces). In a prism, the emergent ray bends at an angle — the angle of deviation (∠D).

🔺 Ray Path Through Prism

1
PE → E (Incident Ray)

Ray enters face AB from air to glass. Bends towards the normal (rarer to denser).

2
EF (Refracted Ray)

Ray travels inside the glass prism from E to F.

3
FS (Emergent Ray)

Ray exits face AC from glass to air. Bends away from the normal (denser to rarer). The emergent ray makes angle ∠D with incident ray direction.

04 · DISPERSION OF WHITE LIGHT

Dispersion by a Glass Prism

🌈

Dispersion

The splitting of white light into its component colours is called dispersion. A prism splits white light into seven colours — VIBGYOR.

🔴

Why Colours Separate

Different colours bend through different angles. Red bends least, violet bends most. Each colour emerges along a different path — they become distinct.

🔬

Newton's Experiment

Isaac Newton was first to use a glass prism to obtain the spectrum of sunlight. He recombined the spectrum using a second inverted prism to get white light back — proving white light is made of 7 colours.

🌧️

Rainbow

A rainbow is a natural spectrum in the sky after rain. Tiny water droplets act like small prisms — they refract, disperse, internally reflect and refract again. Always formed opposite to the Sun.

05 · ATMOSPHERIC REFRACTION

Atmospheric Refraction

💡 Atmospheric Refraction: Refraction of light by the earth's atmosphere. The atmosphere has layers of different densities — different refractive indices. Since physical conditions are not stationary, the apparent position of objects fluctuates.

✨

Twinkling of Stars

Starlight undergoes continuous refraction as it passes through the atmosphere of changing refractive index. The apparent position of the star keeps fluctuating — the amount of light entering the eye flickers. Hence stars twinkle.

Stars are very distant → point-sized sources → small variations in light path appear large.

🪐

Planets Don't Twinkle

Planets are much closer to earth — seen as extended sources. Total variation from all individual point-sized sources averages out to zero — nullifying the twinkling effect.

🌅

Advance Sunrise & Delayed Sunset

The Sun is visible about 2 minutes before actual sunrise and about 2 minutes after actual sunset due to atmospheric refraction. The apparent flattening of the Sun's disc at sunrise/sunset is also due to this.

06 · SCATTERING OF LIGHT

Scattering of Light & Tyndall Effect

⚡ Tyndall Effect

When a beam of light strikes fine particles in a heterogeneous medium (smoke, dust, water droplets), the path of the beam becomes visible. This is the Tyndall Effect.

🔦 Examples: A fine beam of sunlight entering a smoke-filled room through a small hole. Sunlight through a canopy of a dense forest (water droplets in mist scatter light).

📏 Colour depends on particle size: Very fine particles scatter mainly blue light (shorter wavelengths). Larger particles scatter light of longer wavelengths. If particles are large enough, scattered light may appear white.

🌤️ Why is the Sky Blue?

Molecules of air and fine particles in the atmosphere are smaller than the wavelength of visible light. They scatter shorter wavelengths (blue) more strongly than longer wavelengths (red).

1
Blue light scattered strongly

Fine particles scatter blue (shorter wavelength) more strongly. Scattered blue light enters our eyes → sky appears blue.

2
No atmosphere → Dark sky

If the earth had no atmosphere, there would be no scattering → sky would appear dark. This is why the sky appears dark to astronauts at very high altitudes.

3
Red — Danger signal colour

Red light is least scattered by fog or smoke → can be seen at a distance in the same colour. Hence red is used for danger signals.

4
Reddening of Sun at sunrise/sunset

At sunrise/sunset, sunlight travels through a greater thickness of atmosphere. Blue light is scattered away, leaving predominantly red/orange → Sun appears reddish.

IN-TEXT · PAGE 164 Power of Accommodation & Defects of Vision
  1. What is meant by power of accommodation of the eye?
    The power of accommodation is the ability of the eye lens to adjust its focal length to focus objects at different distances clearly on the retina.

    • When looking at distant objects: ciliary muscles relax → lens becomes thin → focal length increases.
    • When looking at nearby objects: ciliary muscles contract → lens becomes thick → focal length decreases.

    This adjustment allows us to see both near and distant objects clearly.
  2. A person with a myopic eye cannot see objects beyond 1.2 m distinctly. What should be the type of the corrective lens used to restore proper vision?
    The person should use a concave lens (diverging lens) of suitable power.

    In myopia, the image of distant objects is formed in front of the retina. A concave lens diverges the incoming rays so that the image falls exactly on the retina.

    The focal length required: The far point is 1.2 m, so the concave lens must form a virtual image of an object at infinity at 1.2 m.
    P = 1/f = 1/(−1.2) ≈ −0.83 D
  3. What is the far point and near point of the human eye with normal vision?
    For a normal eye:
    • Near Point = 25 cm (the least distance of distinct vision — closest point at which objects can be seen clearly without strain).
    • Far Point = Infinity (the farthest point up to which the eye can see objects clearly).

    A normal eye can see objects clearly at distances between 25 cm and infinity.
  4. A student has difficulty reading the blackboard while sitting in the last row. What could be the defect the child is suffering from? How can it be corrected?
    The child is suffering from Myopia (near-sightedness).

    Cause: The image of distant objects (the blackboard) is being formed in front of the retina instead of on it. This may be due to (i) excessive curvature of the eye lens, or (ii) elongation of the eyeball.

    Correction: By using a concave lens (diverging lens) of appropriate power. The concave lens diverges the rays from distant objects so that the image falls exactly on the retina.

  5. A person needs a lens of power −5.5 dioptres for correcting his distant vision. For correcting his near vision he needs a lens of power +1.5 dioptre. What is the focal length of the lens required for correcting (i) distant vision, and (ii) near vision?
    Using f = 1/P (where P is power in dioptres and f is focal length in metres):

    (i) Distant vision:
    P = −5.5 D
    f = 1/P = 1/(−5.5) = −0.182 m ≈ −18.2 cm
    The lens is concave (negative focal length).

    (ii) Near vision:
    P = +1.5 D
    f = 1/P = 1/(+1.5) = +0.667 m ≈ +66.7 cm
    The lens is convex (positive focal length).
  6. The far point of a myopic person is 80 cm in front of the eye. What is the nature and power of the lens required to correct the problem?
    The far point is 80 cm = 0.80 m. The corrective lens must form a virtual image of an object at infinity at the far point (80 cm in front of the eye).

    Using lens formula with: u = ∞, v = −80 cm = −0.80 m (virtual image on same side as object):
    1/f = 1/v − 1/u = 1/(−0.80) − 1/∞ = −1.25 D

    Nature: Concave (diverging) lens
    Power: P = −1.25 D
  7. Make a diagram to show how hypermetropia is corrected. The near point of a hypermetropic eye is 1 m. What is the power of the lens required to correct this defect? Assume that the near point of the normal eye is 25 cm.
    A convex lens is used. The lens must form a virtual image of an object at the normal near point (25 cm) at the hypermetropic near point (1 m = 100 cm).

    Object distance: u = −25 cm = −0.25 m
    Image distance: v = −1 m (virtual image on same side as object, at hypermetropic near point)

    Using lens formula:
    1/f = 1/v − 1/u = 1/(−1) − 1/(−0.25) = −1 + 4 = +3 D

    Power required: P = +3 D (convex lens)
  8. Why is a normal eye not able to see clearly the objects placed closer than 25 cm?
    The focal length of the eye lens cannot be decreased below a certain minimum limit. When an object is placed closer than 25 cm, the ciliary muscles cannot increase the curvature of the lens beyond the maximum possible — so the lens cannot adjust its focal length enough to form a sharp image on the retina.

    The image would form behind the retina, causing blurring and eye strain. 25 cm is therefore the least distance of distinct vision — the nearest point at which the eye can focus without strain.
  9. What happens to the image distance in the eye when we increase the distance of an object from the eye?
    When we increase the distance of an object from the eye, the image distance remains essentially constant at the retinal distance (approximately 2.3 cm for the normal eye).

    To maintain a sharp image on the retina as the object moves farther, the eye adjusts by decreasing the curvature of the lens (ciliary muscles relax) — making the lens thinner and increasing its focal length. This keeps the image precisely on the retina.
  10. Why do stars twinkle?
    Stars twinkle because of atmospheric refraction of starlight.

    When starlight enters the earth's atmosphere, it undergoes continuous refraction through layers of air with gradually changing refractive index. Since the physical conditions of the atmosphere are not stationary, the apparent position of the star keeps changing slightly.

    Since stars are very distant and approximate point-sized sources, even slight changes in the path of light cause:
    • The apparent position to fluctuate.
    • The amount of starlight entering the eye to flicker.
    → The star sometimes appears brighter and sometimes fainter — the twinkling effect.
  11. Explain why the planets do not twinkle.
    Planets do not twinkle because they are much closer to the earth than stars, and are therefore seen as extended sources (having a disc-like appearance) rather than point sources.

    If we consider a planet as a collection of a large number of point-sized sources of light, the total variation in the amount of light entering our eye from all the individual point-sized sources will average out to zero, thereby nullifying the twinkling effect.

    Stars, being extremely distant, appear as point sources, so even tiny fluctuations become significant.
  12. Why does the sky appear dark instead of blue to an astronaut?
    The blue colour of the sky is due to the scattering of sunlight by fine particles in the earth's atmosphere. Blue light (shorter wavelength) is scattered most strongly by these particles and reaches our eyes, making the sky appear blue.

    An astronaut at very high altitudes is above most of the atmosphere. Scattering is not prominent at such heights because there are very few air molecules or fine particles to scatter the light. Without scattering, the sky appears dark (black).

    There is no scattered blue light to reach the astronaut's eyes.
