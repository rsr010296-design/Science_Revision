01 · CHEMICAL PROPERTIES

Properties of Acids and Bases

🍋

Acids

Sour in taste. Turn blue litmus red. Produce H⁺(aq) ions in water. Conduct electricity in aqueous solution.

Examples: HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃, CH₃COOH (acetic acid)

🧼

Bases

Bitter in taste, soapy to touch. Turn red litmus blue. Produce OH⁻(aq) ions in water. Bases soluble in water are called alkalis.

Examples: NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂, Mg(OH)₂

🔬

Indicators

Substances that change colour in acidic or basic media. Litmus (natural), phenolphthalein and methyl orange (synthetic) are common indicators.

Olfactory indicators: Onion, clove oil, vanilla

01.1 · REACTIONS OF ACIDS

Acids React with Metals, Carbonates & Bases

1
Acid + Metal → Salt + Hydrogen gas ↑

The metal displaces hydrogen from the acid. Hydrogen gas burns with a pop sound.

Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)↑
2Na(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂(g)↑
2
Acid + Metal Carbonate → Salt + CO₂ + Water

CO₂ gas turns lime water milky. This is used to test for CO₂.

Na₂CO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)↑
NaHCO₃(s) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)↑
3
Acid + Base → Salt + Water (Neutralisation)

The effect of an acid is nullified by a base and vice versa. H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O

NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)
4
Metal Oxide + Acid → Salt + Water

Metallic oxides are basic oxides. Copper oxide + HCl gives blue-green copper chloride solution.

CuO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CuCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l)
5
Base + Metal → Salt + Hydrogen gas ↑

Not all metals react with bases. Zinc reacts with NaOH to form sodium zincate.

2NaOH(aq) + Zn(s) → Na₂ZnO₂(aq) + H₂(g)↑
6
Non-metallic Oxide + Base → Salt + Water

Non-metallic oxides are acidic in nature. CO₂ reacts with Ca(OH)₂ (lime water).

Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s)↓ + H₂O(l)

02 · IONS IN SOLUTION

What do Acids and Bases have in Common?

🔴 Acids produce H⁺(aq) ions

All acids dissociate in water to produce hydronium ions (H₃O⁺). This is what makes them acidic.

DISSOCIATION IN WATER
HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻
H₂SO₄ + 2H₂O → 2H₃O⁺ + SO₄²⁻
H⁺ ions cannot exist alone — they always exist as H₃O⁺ (hydronium ion)

⚠ Key NCERT point: Dry HCl gas does NOT show acidic behaviour because H⁺ ions are produced only in the presence of water.

⚠ Why dilute acid in water? Always add acid to water (not water to acid). Dissolving acid is highly exothermic — adding water to acid can cause splashing and burns.

🔵 Bases produce OH⁻(aq) ions

All bases dissociate in water to produce hydroxide ions. Bases soluble in water are called alkalis.

DISSOCIATION IN WATER
NaOH → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
KOH → K⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
Mg(OH)₂ → Mg²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq)

💡 Alkali = a base that dissolves in water. They are soapy to touch, bitter and corrosive. All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis.

🧪 Dilution: Mixing an acid or base with water decreases the concentration of H₃O⁺/OH⁻ ions per unit volume. This process is called dilution.

03 · pH SCALE

The pH Scale — Measuring Strength

📉

pH < 7

Acidic solution. Higher the H⁺ concentration, lower the pH. pH = 1 is very strongly acidic.

⚖️

pH = 7

Neutral solution. Pure water has pH = 7. Equal concentrations of H⁺ and OH⁻.

📈

pH > 7

Basic (alkaline) solution. Higher the OH⁻ concentration, higher the pH. pH = 14 is very strongly basic.

💡 What is pH? pH stands for 'potenz' (German for power). The pH scale (0–14) measures hydrogen ion concentration. A universal indicator shows different colours at different pH values. Higher H₃O⁺ concentration → lower pH value.

03.1 · IMPORTANCE

pH in Everyday Life

🦷
Tooth Decay

Tooth decay starts when the pH of the mouth falls below 5.5. Bacteria present in the mouth produce acids by degradation of sugar and food particles.

Tooth enamel is made of calcium hydroxyapatite — the hardest substance in the body. It does not dissolve in water but is corroded at pH below 5.5.

Remedy: Use basic toothpastes to neutralise excess acid.
🌧️
Acid Rain

When the pH of rain water is less than 5.6, it is called acid rain. It flows into rivers, lowering their pH and making survival of aquatic life difficult.

Our body works within the pH range of 7.0 to 7.8. Living organisms can survive only in a narrow range of pH change.

Normal rain: pH ~5.6 | Acid rain: pH < 5.6
🍽️
Indigestion

Our stomach produces hydrochloric acid which helps in digestion. During indigestion, the stomach produces too much acid causing pain and irritation.

People use antacids (bases) to neutralise the excess acid. Magnesium hydroxide (Milk of magnesia) is a mild base commonly used.

Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O
🐝
Bee Sting

A bee sting injects an acid (methanoic acid/formic acid) which causes pain and irritation. Applying a mild base like baking soda gives relief.

Stinging hair of nettle leaves also inject methanoic acid causing burning pain. The traditional remedy is rubbing with the dock plant leaf (basic in nature).

Bee sting: Methanoic acid (HCOOH)
🌱
Soil pH

Plants require a specific pH range for healthy growth. If the soil is too acidic, farmers treat it with quick lime (CaO), slaked lime Ca(OH)₂ or chalk (CaCO₃).

The universal indicator paper is used to measure soil pH.

Ideal soil pH for most crops: 6.0 – 7.0
04 · SALTS

More About Salts

pH of Salts

The pH of a salt depends on the strength of the acid and base used to make it.

SALT OF STRONG ACID + STRONG BASE
NaCl (from HCl + NaOH) → pH = 7 (Neutral)
SALT OF STRONG ACID + WEAK BASE
NH₄Cl (from HCl + NH₄OH) → pH < 7 (Acidic)
SALT OF WEAK ACID + STRONG BASE
Na₂CO₃ (from H₂CO₃ + NaOH) → pH > 7 (Basic)

Family of Salts

Salts having the same positive or negative radicals are said to belong to a family.

🧂 Sodium family: NaCl, Na₂SO₄, Na₂CO₃, NaHCO₃ (all have Na⁺)

🧂 Chloride family: NaCl, KCl, MgCl₂, CaCl₂ (all have Cl⁻)

🧂 Sulphate family: Na₂SO₄, CuSO₄, ZnSO₄, MgSO₄ (all have SO₄²⁻)

💡 Water of Crystallisation: The fixed number of water molecules present in one formula unit of a salt. E.g., CuSO₄·5H₂O (blue crystals → white on heating)

NaHCO₃

Baking Soda

Sodium Hydrogencarbonate. Mild, non-corrosive basic salt. Used in cooking, antacids, fire extinguishers.

Na₂CO₃·10H₂O

Washing Soda

Sodium Carbonate decahydrate. Basic salt. Used in glass, soap, paper industries. Removes hardness of water.

Ca(OH)₂

Slaked Lime

Calcium Hydroxide. Used for whitewashing walls, making cement. Reacts slowly with CO₂ to form CaCO₃.

Ca(ClO)₂

Bleaching Powder

Calcium hypochlorite. Used for bleaching textiles, disinfecting water, oxidising agent.

CuSO₄·5H₂O

Blue Vitriol

Hydrated copper sulphate. Blue crystals. Turns white (anhydrous) on heating. Blue colour restored on adding water.

05 · CHEMICALS FROM COMMON SALT

NaCl — The Raw Material

🧂 Common salt (NaCl) is an important raw material for sodium hydroxide, baking soda, washing soda, bleaching powder and many more chemicals of daily use.

1
Chlor-Alkali Process → NaOH + Cl₂ + H₂

When electricity is passed through an aqueous solution of NaCl (brine), it decomposes. This is called the chlor-alkali process. Cl₂ at anode, H₂ at cathode, NaOH near cathode.

2NaCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + Cl₂(g)↑ + H₂(g)↑
2
Bleaching Powder → Ca(ClO)₂

Chlorine gas from chlor-alkali process reacts with dry slaked lime Ca(OH)₂ to form bleaching powder. Used for bleaching cotton/linen, disinfecting water.

2Ca(OH)₂ + 2Cl₂ → Ca(ClO)₂ + CaCl₂ + 2H₂O
3
Baking Soda → NaHCO₃

Sodium hydrogencarbonate. Produced from NaCl. On heating → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂. Used in cooking (CO₂ makes bread soft and spongy), antacids, fire extinguishers.

NaCl + H₂O + CO₂ + NH₃ → NH₄Cl + NaHCO₃
2NaHCO₃ →(heat) Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂
4
Washing Soda → Na₂CO₃·10H₂O

Recrystallisation of sodium carbonate gives washing soda. Basic salt. Used in glass, soap, paper industries; to remove permanent hardness of water.

Na₂CO₃ + 10H₂O → Na₂CO₃·10H₂O (Washing soda)
5
Plaster of Paris → CaSO₄·½H₂O

Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) heated at 373 K → Plaster of Paris. On mixing with water, it sets hard (reverts to gypsum). Used for fractured bones, toys, smooth surfaces.

CaSO₄·2H₂O →(373K) CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O
CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O (Gypsum)

  1. You have been provided with three test tubes. One of them contains distilled water and the other two contain an acidic solution and a basic solution, respectively. If you are given only red litmus paper, how will you identify the contents of each test tube?
    Dip the red litmus paper into each test tube one by one.
    • The test tube in which the red litmus turns blue → contains the basic solution (base).
    • The test tube in which the red litmus shows no change (stays red) → could be the acidic solution or distilled water.

    To differentiate the remaining two: take a drop from the identified base and put it on a fresh red litmus strip — it turns blue. Now use the base-soaked (blue) litmus paper as a blue litmus: dip it into each of the remaining two solutions.
    • The one that turns the blue litmus red → acidic solution.
    • The one that causes no change → distilled water.

In-Text Questions — Sections 2.1.2 to 2.1.6 (Reactions of Acids & Bases)

Page 22

IN-TEXT · PAGE 22 Reactions of Acids & Bases
  1. Why should curd and sour substances not be kept in brass and copper vessels?
    Curd and sour substances contain acids (e.g. lactic acid, citric acid, tartaric acid). Brass and copper are metals. Acids react with metals to form salts and hydrogen gas:
    Acid + Metal → Salt + H₂↑

    The reaction between the acid in curd/sour substances and the metal of the vessel:
    (i) Dissolves the metal, damaging the vessel.
    (ii) Forms copper or zinc salts which are harmful and can make the food toxic.
    (iii) Changes the taste of the food stored in it.
  2. Which gas is usually liberated when an acid reacts with a metal? Illustrate with an example. How will you test for the presence of this gas?
    Hydrogen gas (H₂) is liberated when an acid reacts with a metal.

    Example:
    Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)↑
    Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)↑

    Test for hydrogen gas: Bring a burning candle near the gas. If the gas burns with a pop sound, it confirms the presence of hydrogen gas.
  3. Metal compound A reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to produce effervescence. The gas evolved extinguishes a burning candle. Write a balanced chemical equation for the reaction if one of the compounds formed is calcium chloride.
    The gas evolved extinguishes a burning candle → it is carbon dioxide (CO₂).
    One product is calcium chloride (CaCl₂) → the metal compound A must contain calcium.
    Therefore, metal compound A = Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃).

    CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)↑

    CO₂ is heavier than air and extinguishes fire, which is why it extinguishes the burning candle.

In-Text Questions — Section 2.2 (Acids & Bases in Common)

Page 25

IN-TEXT · PAGE 25 Ions in Solution & Water
  1. Why do HCl, HNO₃, etc., show acidic characters in aqueous solutions while solutions of compounds like alcohol and glucose do not show acidic character?
    HCl, HNO₃, etc., when dissolved in water, ionise completely to produce H⁺(aq) ions (hydronium ions, H₃O⁺). It is these H⁺ ions that are responsible for their acidic character.

    HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻

    Alcohol and glucose also contain hydrogen but they do not ionise in water to produce H⁺ ions. Since they do not produce H⁺(aq) ions in solution, they do not show acidic character.
  2. Why does an aqueous solution of an acid conduct electricity?
    When an acid is dissolved in water, it ionises to produce ions — positively charged H⁺ (or H₃O⁺) ions and negatively charged anions (Cl⁻, NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, etc.).

    These ions are free to move in the solution. The flow of these charged particles (ions) constitutes an electric current. Hence, an aqueous solution of an acid is a good conductor of electricity.
  3. Why does dry HCl gas not change the colour of the dry litmus paper?
    Acidic behaviour is due to the production of H⁺(aq) ions. H⁺ ions are produced only in the presence of water:

    HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻

    In dry HCl gas, there is no water. Without water, HCl cannot ionise to form H⁺ ions. Since no H⁺(aq) ions are produced, dry HCl gas does not show acidic properties and does not change the colour of dry litmus paper.
  4. While diluting an acid, why is it recommended that the acid should be added to water and not water to the acid?
    The process of dissolving an acid in water is highly exothermic (releases a large amount of heat).

    If water is added to a concentrated acid, the small amount of water heats up very rapidly and may boil, causing the hot acid mixture to splash out and cause serious burns. The glass container may also break due to excessive local heating.

    If the acid is added slowly to a large volume of water, the heat generated is easily absorbed and dissipated by the large amount of water, making the process safe. So, acid is always added to water and not the other way around.
  5. How is the concentration of hydronium ions (H₃O⁺) affected when a solution of an acid is diluted?
    When a solution of an acid is diluted, the concentration of H₃O⁺ ions decreases per unit volume. The volume of the solution increases while the number of H⁺/H₃O⁺ ions remains the same, so there are fewer ions per unit volume.

    This process is called dilution — the acid is said to be diluted. Dilution increases the pH of the acid (moves it closer to 7), making it less acidic.
  6. How is the concentration of hydroxide ions (OH⁻) affected when excess base is dissolved in a solution of sodium hydroxide?
    When excess base is dissolved in a solution of sodium hydroxide (NaOH), more base ionises to produce more OH⁻ ions.

    NaOH → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

    Therefore, the concentration of OH⁻ ions increases. The solution becomes more basic (more alkaline), and its pH value increases (moves further above 7).

In-Text Questions — Section 2.3 (pH Scale)

Page 28

IN-TEXT · PAGE 28 pH Scale & Everyday Life
  1. You have two solutions, A and B. The pH of solution A is 6 and pH of solution B is 8. Which solution has more hydrogen ion concentration? Which of this is acidic and which one is basic?
    Solution A (pH 6) has more hydrogen ion (H⁺) concentration because lower pH means higher H⁺ concentration.

    • Solution A (pH 6) → pH < 7 → Acidic
    • Solution B (pH 8) → pH > 7 → Basic (Alkaline)
  2. What effect does the concentration of H⁺(aq) ions have on the nature of the solution?
    • Higher concentration of H⁺(aq) ions → solution is more acidic → lower pH value (closer to 0).
    • Lower concentration of H⁺(aq) ions → solution is less acidic or neutral or basic → higher pH value (closer to 14).

    In short: Higher [H⁺] → More acidic → Lower pH.
  3. Do basic solutions also have H⁺(aq) ions? If yes, then why are these basic?
    Yes, basic solutions do contain H⁺(aq) ions, but in very small concentrations.

    A solution is basic because the concentration of OH⁻(aq) ions is greater than the concentration of H⁺(aq) ions in it. The excess OH⁻ ions are responsible for the basic character, even though H⁺ ions are present in small amounts.
  4. Under what soil condition do you think a farmer would treat the soil of his fields with quick lime (calcium oxide) or slaked lime (calcium hydroxide) or chalk (calcium carbonate)?
    A farmer would treat the soil with quick lime (CaO), slaked lime [Ca(OH)₂] or chalk (CaCO₃) when the soil is too acidic (has a low pH).

    These substances are basic in nature. When they are added to acidic soil, they neutralise the excess acid in the soil, raising its pH to a level suitable for crop growth. Plants require a specific pH range for healthy growth.

In-Text Questions — Section 2.4 (More About Salts)

Page 33

IN-TEXT · PAGE 33 Chemicals from Common Salt
  1. What is the common name of the compound Ca(ClO)₂?
    The common name of Ca(ClO)₂ is Bleaching powder (also called calcium oxychloride or calcium hypochlorite).

    It is produced by the action of chlorine on dry slaked lime [Ca(OH)₂]:
    2Ca(OH)₂ + 2Cl₂ → Ca(ClO)₂ + CaCl₂ + 2H₂O
  2. Name the substance which on treatment with chlorine yields bleaching powder.
    Dry slaked lime — Ca(OH)₂ (calcium hydroxide) on treatment with chlorine yields bleaching powder.

    2Ca(OH)₂ + 2Cl₂ → Ca(ClO)₂ + CaCl₂ + 2H₂O

    The chlorine used here is produced during the chlor-alkali process (electrolysis of brine).
  3. Name the sodium compound which is used for softening hard water.
    Washing soda — Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O) is used for softening hard water.

    Hard water contains dissolved salts of calcium and magnesium. Washing soda (Na₂CO₃) reacts with these salts to form insoluble carbonates which can be filtered off, thus removing the hardness of water.
  4. What will happen if a solution of sodium hydrogencarbonate is heated? Give the equation of the reaction involved.
    When sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃) solution is heated, it decomposes to give sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃), water and carbon dioxide gas. Brisk effervescence is observed due to the evolution of CO₂.

    2NaHCO₃ →(heat) Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂↑
  5. Write an equation to show the reaction between Plaster of Paris and water.
    When Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O) is mixed with water, it reacts to form gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) and becomes hard (sets).

    CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O (Gypsum)

    This property of setting hard is used in making casts for fractured bones, toys and smooth surfaces.

  6. Compounds such as alcohols and glucose also contain hydrogen but are not categorised as acids. Describe an Activity to prove it.
    Activity (as per NCERT Activity 2.8):
    Fix two nails on a cork and place it in a 100 mL beaker. Connect the nails to the two terminals of a 6 volt battery through a bulb and a switch.

    Procedure:
    1. Pour dilute HCl in the beaker and switch on the current → bulb glows (HCl ionises to give H⁺ and Cl⁻ ions that conduct electricity).
    2. Repeat with dilute H₂SO₄ → bulb glows.
    3. Now try with glucose solution → bulb does NOT glow.
    4. Try with alcohol solution → bulb does NOT glow.

    Conclusion: Acids (HCl, H₂SO₄) produce H⁺(aq) ions in water which conduct electricity. Alcohol and glucose do not ionise in water, produce no H⁺ ions, and hence are not acids despite containing hydrogen.
  7. Why does distilled water not conduct electricity, whereas rain water does?
    Distilled water is pure water — it contains no dissolved salts, acids or bases, and therefore has no free ions. Without ions, electric current cannot flow through it. Hence, distilled water is a poor conductor of electricity.

    Rain water, as it falls through the atmosphere, dissolves carbon dioxide (CO₂) and other gases present in the air. CO₂ dissolved in water forms carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), which ionises to give H⁺ and HCO₃⁻ ions. These ions can carry electric current. Hence, rain water conducts electricity.
  8. Why does dry HCl gas not show acidic behaviour in the absence of water?
    The acidic behaviour of HCl is due to the production of H⁺(aq) ions. H⁺ ions are produced only when HCl dissolves in water:

    HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻

    In the absence of water, HCl molecules cannot ionise to form H⁺ ions. Without H⁺(aq) ions, HCl cannot show acidic behaviour such as changing the colour of litmus paper or reacting with metals. Therefore, dry HCl gas does not show acidic behaviour.

  10. Equal lengths of magnesium ribbons are taken in test tubes A and B. Hydrochloric acid (HCl) is added to test tube A, while acetic acid (CH₃COOH) is added to test tube B. Amount and concentration taken for both the acids are same. In which test tube will the fizzing occur more vigorously and why?
    Test tube A (HCl) will show more vigorous fizzing.

    Reason: HCl is a strong acid — it ionises almost completely in water to produce a high concentration of H⁺(aq) ions. Acetic acid (CH₃COOH) is a weak acid — it ionises only partially in water, producing fewer H⁺(aq) ions.

    Since more H⁺ ions are available in test tube A, the reaction with magnesium is faster and more vigorous, producing H₂ gas (fizzing) at a higher rate.

    Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)↑ (vigorous)
    Mg(s) + 2CH₃COOH → (CH₃COO)₂Mg + H₂(g)↑ (slow)
  11. Fresh milk has a pH of 6. How do you think the pH will change as it turns into curd? Explain your answer.
    The pH of milk will decrease (become more acidic) as it turns into curd.

    Reason: When milk turns into curd, bacteria (mainly Lactobacillus) act on the lactose (sugar) in milk and produce lactic acid. As lactic acid accumulates in the milk, the concentration of H⁺ ions increases, causing the pH to fall below 6. The sour taste of curd is due to this increase in acidity.
  12. A milkman adds a very small amount of baking soda to fresh milk. (a) Why does he shift the pH of the fresh milk from 6 to slightly alkaline? (b) Why does this milk take a long time to set as curd?
    (a) The milkman shifts the pH to slightly alkaline to prevent the milk from turning sour quickly. Fresh milk has a pH of 6 (slightly acidic). As bacteria act on it, they produce lactic acid, lowering the pH further and making the milk sour. By adding baking soda (NaHCO₃, a mild base), the milkman neutralises the slight acidity and makes the milk slightly alkaline, which delays the souring process, allowing the milk to last longer.

    (b) Milk turns into curd when its pH drops sufficiently (due to lactic acid production by bacteria) for the proteins to coagulate. Since baking soda has made the milk slightly alkaline, the bacteria need to produce more lactic acid to lower the pH to the required level. This takes more time. Hence, this milk takes a longer time to set as curd.
  13. Plaster of Paris should be stored in a moisture-proof container. Explain why?
    Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O) readily absorbs moisture (water) from the atmosphere and reacts with it to form gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O), which is a hard solid:

    CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O (Gypsum)

    If moisture enters the container, Plaster of Paris will prematurely set and harden in the container, making it completely useless for any future application. To prevent this, it must be stored in a moisture-proof (airtight) container.
  14. What is a neutralisation reaction? Give two examples.
    The reaction between an acid and a base to give a salt and water is called a neutralisation reaction. The effect of an acid is nullified by the base and vice-versa. In ionic terms:
    H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)

    Example 1:
    NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)
    (Sodium hydroxide + Hydrochloric acid → Sodium chloride + Water)

    Example 2:
    Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O
    (Milk of magnesia neutralises excess HCl in stomach — antacid action)
  15. Give two important uses of washing soda and baking soda.
    Washing soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O — Sodium carbonate decahydrate):
    1. Used in glass, soap and paper industries.
    2. Used for removing permanent hardness of water (reacts with Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ salts to form insoluble carbonates).
    (Also used as a cleaning agent for domestic purposes and in the manufacture of sodium compounds such as borax.)

    Baking soda (NaHCO₃ — Sodium hydrogencarbonate):
    1. Used in baking — when heated or mixed in water it releases CO₂ which makes bread and cake soft and spongy.
    2. Used as an antacid — being alkaline, it neutralises excess acid in the stomach and provides relief from indigestion.
    (Also used in soda-acid fire extinguishers.)
