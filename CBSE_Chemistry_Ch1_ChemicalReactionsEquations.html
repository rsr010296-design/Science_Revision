01 · CHEMICAL EQUATIONS

Writing & Balancing Chemical Equations

📝

Word Equation

A chemical reaction written in sentence form. Reactants are on the left-hand side (LHS) and products on the right-hand side (RHS) separated by an arrow (→).

Magnesium + Oxygen → Magnesium oxide

⚖️

Balanced Equation

Based on the Law of Conservation of Mass — the number of atoms of each element must be the same on both sides. A skeletal equation that is not balanced is incomplete.

3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g)

🔬

Physical State Symbols

Added to make equations more informative: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) aqueous solution. Reaction conditions written above/below the arrow.

2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)

📋 Signs of a Chemical Reaction (as per NCERT)

🌈 Change in colour
e.g. iron nail turns brown in CuSO₄ solution

💨 Evolution of a gas
e.g. H₂ gas when Zn reacts with H₂SO₄

🌡️ Change in temperature
e.g. heat released in CaO + H₂O reaction

🧊 Change in state
e.g. solid CaCO₃ gives gaseous CO₂ on heating

⬇️ Formation of precipitate
e.g. white BaSO₄ precipitate in double displacement

⚖️ Steps to Balance a Chemical Equation — Hit and Trial Method

Example: Balancing Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂

I
Write the skeletal equation

Write the unbalanced equation using correct chemical formulae. Draw boxes around each formula.

Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂
II
Count atoms on each side

List the number of atoms of each element on LHS and RHS. Here: Fe: 1 vs 3 | H: 2 vs 2 | O: 1 vs 4 → not balanced.

III
Balance the atom with maximum count first

Start with Fe₃O₄ (maximum atoms). Balance O: put coefficient 4 before H₂O.

Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂ (partly balanced)
IV
Balance H atoms

Now 8 H atoms on LHS (4H₂O), so put 4 before H₂ on RHS.

Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂ (partly balanced)
V
Balance remaining element (Fe)

1 Fe on LHS, 3 on RHS → put 3 before Fe.

3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂ ✓ (balanced)
VI
Add physical state symbols

Final balanced equation with state symbols:

3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g)

💡 Law of Conservation of Mass (NCERT): Mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. The total mass of elements present in products = total mass of elements present in reactants. Hence we must always balance chemical equations.

02 · TYPES OF CHEMICAL REACTIONS

Five Types of Chemical Reactions

🔗 1. Combination Reaction

A reaction in which two or more substances combine to form a single product. General form: A + B → AB

COMBINATION · EXOTHERMIC
CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + Heat
Quick lime + water → Slaked lime (releases large heat)
BURNING OF COAL
C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)
FORMATION OF WATER
2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)

🔥 Exothermic Reactions — reactions in which heat is released along with the formation of products.

Examples: burning of natural gas, respiration (C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy), decomposition of vegetable matter into compost.

💡 Slaked lime on walls: Ca(OH)₂ slowly reacts with CO₂ in air to form CaCO₃ (calcium carbonate), giving walls a shiny finish. Marble also has the formula CaCO₃.

⚡ 2. Decomposition Reaction

A reaction in which a single substance breaks down into two or more simpler substances. Opposite of combination. General form: AB → A + B. Requires energy (heat/light/electricity).

🌡
Thermal Decomposition (Heat)
2FeSO₄(s) →(heat) Fe₂O₃(s) + SO₂(g) + SO₃(g)
CaCO₃(s) →(heat) CaO(s) + CO₂(g) ↑
2Pb(NO₃)₂(s) →(heat) 2PbO(s) + 4NO₂(g) + O₂(g)

FeSO₄: green → brown (Fe₂O₃). CaCO₃: used in industry to make quick lime (CaO).

💡
Photolytic Decomposition (Light)
2AgCl(s) →(sunlight) 2Ag(s) + Cl₂(g)
2AgBr(s) →(sunlight) 2Ag(s) + Br₂(g)

Used in black and white photography. White AgCl turns grey in sunlight.

⚡
Electrolytic Decomposition (Electricity)
2H₂O(l) →(electricity) 2H₂(g) + O₂(g)

Electrolysis of water. H₂ collected at cathode, O₂ at anode. Volume of H₂ is double that of O₂ (ratio 2:1).

❄️ Endothermic Reactions — decomposition reactions require energy (heat, light or electricity) to break down reactants. Reactions in which energy is absorbed are called endothermic reactions.

↔️ 3. Displacement Reaction

A reaction in which a more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its compound. General form: A + BC → AC + B

IRON DISPLACES COPPER
Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)
Iron nail turns brown; blue colour of CuSO₄ fades
ZINC DISPLACES COPPER
Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)
LEAD DISPLACES COPPER
Pb(s) + CuCl₂(aq) → PbCl₂(aq) + Cu(s)

⚡ Reactivity order matters: Zinc and lead are more reactive than copper — they displace copper from its compounds. Iron is also more reactive than copper.

In Activity 1.9: Iron nails dipped in CuSO₄ solution — the iron nail becomes brownish (copper deposited) and the blue colour of CuSO₄ fades (copper displaced).

🔄 4. Double Displacement Reaction

Reactions in which two different atoms or groups of atoms (ions) are exchanged between the reactants. Usually produces an insoluble precipitate. General form: AB + CD → AD + CB

PRECIPITATION REACTION
Na₂SO₄(aq) + BaCl₂(aq) → BaSO₄(s)↓ + 2NaCl(aq)
White precipitate of BaSO₄ formed — insoluble in water. SO₄²⁻ and Ba²⁺ ions exchange.
LEAD NITRATE + POTASSIUM IODIDE
Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s)↓ + 2KNO₃(aq)
Yellow precipitate of lead iodide (PbI₂) formed. Also a double displacement reaction.

💡 Precipitation reactions produce insoluble salts (precipitates). Any reaction that produces a precipitate can be called a precipitation reaction.

03 · OXIDATION & REDUCTION

Oxidation, Reduction & Redox Reactions

🔴 Oxidation

A substance is oxidised if it gains oxygen OR loses hydrogen.

COPPER OXIDISED
2Cu(s) + O₂(g) →(heat) 2CuO(s)
Copper gains oxygen → oxidised. Black CuO coating formed.
MAGNESIUM OXIDISED
2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)
Magnesium burns with dazzling white flame — gains oxygen, is oxidised.

📌 Other examples:
In ZnO + C → Zn + CO: Carbon is oxidised (gains O to form CO).
In MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + 2H₂O + Cl₂: HCl is oxidised (loses H to form Cl₂).

🔵 Reduction

A substance is reduced if it loses oxygen OR gains hydrogen.

COPPER OXIDE REDUCED
CuO(s) + H₂(g) →(heat) Cu(s) + H₂O(l)
CuO loses oxygen → reduced (black turns brown). H₂ gains oxygen → oxidised.
ZINC OXIDE REDUCED
ZnO(s) + C(s) → Zn(s) + CO(g)
ZnO loses oxygen → reduced to Zn. Carbon gains oxygen → oxidised to CO.

📌 Remember:
In MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + 2H₂O + Cl₂: MnO₂ is reduced (loses O to form MnCl₂).

⚗️ Redox Reactions — Oxidation & Reduction Occur Simultaneously

In a redox reaction, one reactant gets oxidised while the other gets reduced. They always occur together.

REDOX EXAMPLE (CuO + H₂)
CuO + H₂ →(heat) Cu + H₂O
↑ CuO loses oxygen → REDUCED
↑ H₂ gains oxygen → OXIDISED

📝 NCERT Definition Summary:
• Oxidised = gains oxygen OR loses hydrogen
• Reduced = loses oxygen OR gains hydrogen
• Redox reaction = oxidation and reduction happen simultaneously in the same reaction

04 · OXIDATION IN EVERYDAY LIFE

Corrosion & Rancidity

🦀 Corrosion

When a metal is attacked by substances around it such as moisture, acids, etc., it is said to corrode and this process is called corrosion.

1
Rusting of Iron

Iron articles become coated with a reddish-brown powder when left in humid atmosphere. Iron reacts with oxygen and water to form iron oxide (rust).

2
Black coating on Silver

Silver reacts with sulphur compounds in air to form black silver sulphide (Ag₂S) — tarnishing.

3
Green coating on Copper

Copper reacts with CO₂ and moisture to form green basic copper carbonate — verdigris.

⚠️ Prevention: Painting iron articles prevents contact with moisture and oxygen, preventing rusting. Galvanising, alloying and electroplating are other methods.

🍟 Rancidity

When fats and oils in food are oxidised, they become rancid — their smell and taste change. This is called rancidity.

1
Oxidation of Fats/Oils

Fats and oils undergo oxidation when exposed to air, producing foul-smelling compounds. Food left for a long time becomes rancid.

2
Antioxidants

Substances that prevent oxidation (antioxidants) are added to foods containing fats and oil to slow down rancidity.

3
Nitrogen Flushing

Chips manufacturers flush bags with nitrogen gas — nitrogen is unreactive and prevents oxidation of chips.

4
Airtight Containers

Keeping food in airtight containers helps slow down oxidation and prevents rancidity.

🔥
Respiration — Exothermic

Respiration is an exothermic process. Glucose from food is broken down with oxygen to produce energy, CO₂ and water.

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy
📷
Photography

Black and white photography uses the photolytic decomposition of silver chloride (AgCl) and silver bromide (AgBr) by light to form silver metal.

2AgCl →(sunlight) 2Ag + Cl₂ (white → grey)
🏗️
Cement Industry

Calcium carbonate (limestone) is thermally decomposed to produce calcium oxide (quick lime), used in the manufacture of cement.

CaCO₃ →(heat) CaO + CO₂↑ (thermal decomposition)
🪣
Whitewashing

Slaked lime solution Ca(OH)₂ reacts slowly with CO₂ in air to form CaCO₃, giving walls a shiny finish after 2–3 days.

Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O (combination)
⚡
Electrolysis of Water

Water is decomposed by electricity into hydrogen (at cathode) and oxygen (at anode). Volume of H₂ collected is double that of O₂ (2:1 ratio).

2H₂O →(electricity) 2H₂ + O₂ (electrolytic decomposition)
🥈
Silver Refining

Recovery of silver from silver nitrate solution involves displacement by copper metal. Copper is more reactive than silver.

Cu + 2AgNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2Ag (displacement)

Keywords & Definitions (as per NCERT)

KEYWORDNCERT DEFINITION
Chemical ReactionA process in which the nature and identity of a substance changes. Signs: change in state, colour, evolution of gas, change in temperature, or formation of precipitate.
ReactantsThe substances that undergo chemical change in a reaction. Written on the left-hand side (LHS) of the arrow.
ProductsNew substances formed during a chemical reaction. Written on the right-hand side (RHS) of the arrow.
Skeletal EquationA chemical equation that is not balanced — the number of atoms of each element is not equal on both sides.
Balanced EquationA chemical equation where the number of atoms of each element is the same on both the reactant and product sides. Based on Law of Conservation of Mass.
Law of Conservation of MassMass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. Total mass of reactants = total mass of products.
Combination ReactionA reaction in which two or more substances combine to form a single product. General: A + B → AB.
Decomposition ReactionA reaction in which a single substance breaks down into two or more simpler substances. Opposite of combination. General: AB → A + B.
Exothermic ReactionReactions in which heat is released along with the formation of products. Example: burning of natural gas, respiration, formation of slaked lime.
Endothermic ReactionReactions in which energy is absorbed. Decomposition reactions require energy (heat, light or electricity) and are endothermic.
Displacement ReactionA reaction in which a more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its compound. General: A + BC → AC + B.
Double Displacement ReactionA reaction in which two different atoms or groups of atoms (ions) are exchanged between the reactants. General: AB + CD → AD + CB.

OxidationGain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen by a substance during a reaction.
ReductionLoss of oxygen or gain of hydrogen by a substance during a reaction.
Redox ReactionA reaction in which one reactant gets oxidised while the other gets reduced simultaneously. Also called oxidation-reduction reaction.
CorrosionWhen a metal is attacked by substances around it (moisture, acids, etc.), it is said to corrode. Example: rusting of iron (reddish-brown), black coating on silver, green coating on copper.
RancidityThe process by which fats and oils in food are oxidised when exposed to air, leading to a change in smell and taste. Prevented by antioxidants, airtight containers, nitrogen flushing.
Thermal DecompositionA decomposition reaction carried out by heating. Example: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂.

Electrolytic DecompositionDecomposition caused by electricity. Example: 2H₂O →(electricity) 2H₂ + O₂.
Hit-and-Trial MethodMethod of balancing chemical equations by trying different coefficients (smallest whole numbers) until both sides have equal atoms of each element.

  1. Why should a magnesium ribbon be cleaned before burning in air?
    Magnesium ribbon is cleaned before burning to remove the layer of magnesium oxide (MgO) formed on its surface due to exposure to air (oxidation).   1. Why does the colour of copper sulphate solution change when an iron nail is dipped in it?
    Iron (Fe) is more reactive than copper (Cu). When an iron nail is dipped in copper sulphate (CuSO₄) solution, iron displaces copper from the solution:

    Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

    The blue colour of CuSO₄ fades because Cu²⁺ ions are replaced by Fe²⁺ ions (FeSO₄ is light green/colourless). Copper metal deposits on the iron nail, making it brownish in colour.
  2. Give an example of a double displacement reaction other than the one given in Activity 1.10.
    Example 1: Lead nitrate reacts with potassium iodide:
    Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s)↓ + 2KNO₃(aq)
    (Yellow precipitate of lead iodide formed)

    Example 2: Silver nitrate with sodium chloride:
    AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s)↓ + NaNO₃(aq)
    (White precipitate of silver chloride formed)
  3. Identify the substances that are oxidised and the substances that are reduced in the following reactions:
    (i) 4Na(s) + O₂(g) → 2Na₂O(s)
    (ii) CuO(s) + H₂(g) → Cu(s) + H₂O(l)
    (i) 4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O
    • Na is oxidised — sodium gains oxygen to form Na₂O.
    • (O₂ is the oxidising agent; it helps oxidise Na)

    (ii) CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O
    • CuO is reduced — copper oxide loses oxygen (Cu is freed from CuO).
    • H₂ is oxidised — hydrogen gains oxygen to form H₂O.

  9. What does one mean by exothermic and endothermic reactions? Give examples.
    Exothermic Reactions: Reactions in which heat is released along with the formation of products.
    Examples:
    • CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + Heat (slaked lime formation)
    • CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g) + energy (burning of natural gas)
    • C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy (respiration)

    Endothermic Reactions: Reactions in which energy is absorbed from the surroundings.
    Examples:
    • 2AgCl(s) →(sunlight) 2Ag(s) + Cl₂(g) (photolytic decomposition)
    • CaCO₃(s) →(heat) CaO(s) + CO₂(g) (thermal decomposition of limestone)
    • 2H₂O(l) →(electricity) 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) (electrolysis of water)
  10. Why is respiration considered an exothermic reaction? Explain.
    Respiration is considered an exothermic reaction because it releases energy along with the formation of products.

    During digestion, carbohydrates (like rice, potatoes, bread) are broken down into glucose. This glucose combines with oxygen in the cells of our body and provides energy:

    C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(aq) → 6CO₂(aq) + 6H₂O(l) + energy

    Since heat (energy) is released during this process, respiration is an exothermic reaction. We need this energy to stay alive and perform all biological functions.
  11. Why are decomposition reactions called the opposite of combination reactions? Write equations for these reactions.
    In a combination reaction, two or more substances combine to form a single product: A + B → AB
    In a decomposition reaction, a single substance breaks down into two or more simpler substances: AB → A + B

    Since one builds up a compound while the other breaks it down, they are exactly opposite processes.

    Combination: CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + Heat
    Decomposition: Ca(OH)₂(aq) + Heat → CaO(s) + H₂O(l) (reverse of above)

    Another pair:
    Combination: 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)
    Decomposition: 2H₂O(l) →(electricity) 2H₂(g) + O₂(g)
  12. Write one equation each for decomposition reactions where energy is supplied in the form of heat, light or electricity.
    Heat (Thermal decomposition):
    CaCO₃(s) →(heat) CaO(s) + CO₂(g)↑

    Light (Photolytic decomposition):
    2AgCl(s) →(sunlight) 2Ag(s) + Cl₂(g)

    Electricity (Electrolytic decomposition):
    2H₂O(l) →(electricity) 2H₂(g) + O₂(g)
  13. What is the difference between displacement and double displacement reactions? Write equations for these reactions.
    Displacement Reaction: A more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its compound. Only one element is displaced.
    General: A + BC → AC + B
    Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

    Double Displacement Reaction: Two different ions/groups are exchanged between two compounds. A precipitate or water is usually formed.
    General: AB + CD → AD + CB
    Na₂SO₄(aq) + BaCl₂(aq) → BaSO₄(s)↓ + 2NaCl(aq)
  14. In the refining of silver, the recovery of silver from silver nitrate solution involved displacement by copper metal. Write down the reaction involved.
    Copper is more reactive than silver and displaces silver from silver nitrate solution:

    Cu(s) + 2AgNO₃(aq) → Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2Ag(s)

    This is a displacement reaction. Copper displaces silver from AgNO₃ solution. Silver metal is deposited, and copper nitrate solution is formed.
  15. What do you mean by a precipitation reaction? Explain by giving examples.
    A precipitation reaction is one that produces an insoluble solid (called a precipitate) when two solutions are mixed. The precipitate settles at the bottom of the container.

    Example 1: Sodium sulphate + Barium chloride:
    Na₂SO₄(aq) + BaCl₂(aq) → BaSO₄(s)↓ + 2NaCl(aq)
    White precipitate of barium sulphate (BaSO₄) is formed.

    Example 2: Lead nitrate + Potassium iodide:
    Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s)↓ + 2KNO₃(aq)
    Yellow precipitate of lead iodide (PbI₂) is formed.
  16. Explain the following in terms of gain or loss of oxygen with two examples each: (a) Oxidation (b) Reduction
    (a) Oxidation = Gain of oxygen OR loss of hydrogen
    Example 1: 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO  (copper gains oxygen — oxidised)
    Example 2: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO  (magnesium gains oxygen — oxidised)

    (b) Reduction = Loss of oxygen OR gain of hydrogen
    Example 1: CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O  (CuO loses oxygen — reduced)
    Example 2: ZnO + C → Zn + CO  (ZnO loses oxygen — reduced)
  17. A shiny brown coloured element 'X' on heating in air becomes black in colour. Name the element 'X' and the black coloured compound formed.
    Element 'X' is Copper (Cu) — it is shiny brown in colour.

    On heating in air, copper reacts with oxygen to form Copper(II) oxide (CuO), which is black in colour:

    2Cu(s) + O₂(g) →(heat) 2CuO(s)

    This is an oxidation reaction — copper gains oxygen to form the black copper oxide coating.
  18. Why do we apply paint on iron articles?
    We apply paint on iron articles to prevent rusting (corrosion).

    Iron reacts with oxygen and moisture (water) in the air to form reddish-brown iron oxide (rust — Fe₂O₃·xH₂O). This process is called corrosion and causes damage to iron structures.

    Paint forms a protective coating on the surface of iron that prevents contact between iron and the oxygen/moisture in the air. Without this contact, the oxidation reaction (rusting) cannot take place, so the iron article is protected.
  19. Oil and fat containing food items are flushed with nitrogen. Why?
    Oil and fat containing food items are flushed with nitrogen to prevent rancidity.

    When fats and oils are exposed to oxygen in the air, they undergo oxidation and become rancid — their smell and taste change, making the food unfit for consumption.

    Nitrogen (N₂) is a chemically unreactive (inert) gas. When bags are flushed with nitrogen, oxygen is displaced. Without oxygen, the oxidation of fats and oils cannot occur, so the food stays fresh for longer. This is why chips manufacturers flush bags of chips with nitrogen gas.
  20. Explain the following terms with one example each: (a) Corrosion (b) Rancidity
    (a) Corrosion: When a metal is attacked by substances around it such as moisture, acids, etc., the metal is said to corrode and this process is called corrosion.
    Example: Rusting of iron — when iron is exposed to moist air, it reacts with oxygen and water to form reddish-brown iron oxide (rust). This damages iron structures, bridges, railings and ships. Every year an enormous amount of money is spent replacing corroded iron.

    (b) Rancidity: When fats and oils in food are oxidised by exposure to air, their smell and taste change. The food is said to have become rancid. This process is called rancidity.
    Example: Butter kept exposed to air for a long time develops an unpleasant smell and taste due to oxidation of the fats in it. Adding antioxidants or storing in airtight containers prevents rancidity.
